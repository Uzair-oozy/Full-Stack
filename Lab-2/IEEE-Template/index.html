A Practical Framework for Human-Centered
Intelligent Systems

Designing reliable interfaces at the intersection of computation, context, and care

Alex Morgan Department of Computer Science
Northbridge University
Priya Shah School of Information Technology
Northbridge University
Jordan Lee Research & Innovation Lab
Northbridge University
Received 08 January 2025 Revised 21 March 2025 DOI: 10.1109/EXAMPLE.2025.00001

Abstract

Intelligent systems increasingly mediate decisions that affect everyday life, yet many are evaluated primarily through technical performance. This paper presents a practical framework for designing human-centered intelligent systems that balance accuracy, transparency, and user agency. Through a mixed-method evaluation of three prototype interfaces, we identify four design principles that improve comprehension without adding operational friction. The findings suggest that modest changes in feedback timing, explanation granularity, and control placement can produce meaningful gains in trust and task completion.

Index Terms— human-centered computing, intelligent systems, interface design, explainable artificial intelligence, user trust.

I. Introduction

Technical systems are no longer passive tools. They recommend, classify, prioritize, and increasingly act on behalf of their users. This shift creates a design challenge: a system may be statistically effective while remaining difficult to understand or contest.

Human-centered design offers a useful lens for this challenge, but its principles are often stated at a level too general to guide implementation. This work translates those principles into a compact set of interface decisions that can be evaluated in a working prototype.

Our contributions are threefold:

  1. A framework connecting system behavior to visible user controls.
  2. An evaluation protocol for measuring comprehension and agency.
  3. Design recommendations grounded in observed interaction patterns.

II. Methodology

We developed three task-oriented prototypes that varied in explanation depth and control visibility. Twenty-four participants completed a recommendation task and a correction task in a counterbalanced study design.

Ts = A × X Ci (1)

Here, Ts denotes perceived trust, A represents task accuracy, X represents explanation transparency, and Ci is the interaction cost required to inspect or correct a result.

III. Results and Discussion

Participants completed tasks faster when explanations were available at the point of decision rather than in a separate help view. The effect was strongest for unfamiliar recommendations, where participants used the correction control as a signal that the system could be challenged.

Condition Accuracy Time (s) Trust
Baseline 78.2% 42.8 3.1

The full framework improved all measured outcomes relative to the baseline. Qualitative responses indicate that participants did not seek more information in general; they sought the right information at the right moment.

“The system felt more useful when I could see why it made a choice and what I could do about it.”— Participant 17

IV. Conclusion

This paper presented a practical framework for human-centered intelligent systems. The results reinforce a simple premise: user agency is an interface property, not merely a policy statement. Future work will test the framework in high-stakes domains and examine how explanations should adapt to expertise over time.
